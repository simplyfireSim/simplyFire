Combustion science basics

A plain-language summary of what combustion is, why gravity changes it, and how NASA studies it, drawn from NASA’s combustion science pages and the PSI collection this dashboard is built on.

In one sentence. Combustion is a fast chemical reaction in which a fuel and an oxidizer, usually the oxygen in air, release heat and light. Combustion science studies how fires start, spread, burn and go out, and space lets researchers watch them without gravity getting in the way.

What is combustion?

Burning needs three things at once: a fuel, an oxidizer and enough heat to start the reaction. Once it is going, the heat the reaction releases keeps it going, which is why a small flame can grow into a fire. Remove any one of the three and it stops. Extinguishers work that way: they cool the fire, smother it, or dilute the oxygen, as FLEX tested with CO₂ and helium.

Why scientists study it

Combustion sits behind electricity generation, engines and heating, so burning more cleanly and efficiently matters on Earth (the ACME experiments aim at better efficiency and lower emissions). It also matters for safety: in a sealed spacecraft or lunar base a fire cannot be escaped, so crews need to know how materials ignite, how smoke behaves and what puts a fire out.

Kinds of flames and fires

Premixed flames

Fuel and oxidizer are blended before they burn, like a Bunsen burner with its air hole open. The flame front travels through the mixture.

Diffusion flames

Fuel and air meet only at the flame, as in a candle or lighter. Shape and soot depend strongly on gravity. SPICE measures the smoke point and soot formation.

Droplets and sprays

Liquid fuel burns as it evaporates, as in many engines. FLEX burned heptane and methanol droplets and tested what it takes to extinguish them.

Solid materials

Plastics, fabrics and other solids burn as heat turns their surface into flammable gas, and the flame spreads across them. BASS, BASS-II and Saffire measure how, and at what oxygen level.

Smoldering

Slow, flameless burning that glows and smokes, like a cigarette or smoldering insulation. It can go unnoticed. NASA ran microgravity smoldering experiments in the 1990s.

Cool flames

Faint, low-temperature flames that burn through different chemistry than ordinary flames. CFI-G studied them with oxygen up to 85%.

Why gravity changes fire

A flame is about 2,300 K, while the air around it is about 300 K. On Earth that enormous density difference makes hot gas rise (buoyancy), which pulls fresh air into the flame and makes it flicker. In free fall that rising stops, and effects hidden on Earth show up.

How NASA studies it

  1. Ground tests. Small flames in labs, drop towers and aircraft flights give seconds of reduced gravity.
  2. Space station experiments. ISS facilities such as the Combustion Integrated Rack (CIR), the Microgravity Science Glovebox (MSG) and the Multi-user Droplet Combustion Apparatus (MDCA) allow longer, repeatable runs.
  3. Spacecraft-scale fires. The Saffire series burns large samples inside an uncrewed Cygnus vehicle.
  4. Shared data. Results go to the PSI Data Repository so other scientists can reuse them.
  5. Safety rules. Findings feed standards such as NASA-STD-6001, which sets how materials are tested and selected for flammability.
  6. Partial gravity. LUCI burned fabric and acrylic in simulated lunar gravity in 2025, with results in NASA’s Technical Reports Server. FM2 is planned to burn four samples on the Moon itself. PSI’s combustion collection may not contain partial-gravity datasets.

Key words

Oxidizer
The substance that reacts with the fuel, most often oxygen.
Ignition
The moment a fuel and oxidizer start burning on their own.
Flammability limit
The lowest oxygen level, or the leanest mixture, at which something will still burn.
Flame spread
How fast a flame travels across a material.
Diffusion
Slow mixing of gases by random molecular motion, with no flow needed.
Buoyancy
The force that makes hot, light gas rise through cooler, denser gas.
Soot
Tiny carbon particles from incomplete burning; they make flames glow yellow and make smoke.
Extinction
When a flame goes out, by cooling, running out of fuel or oxygen, or dilution.
Microgravity
Near weightlessness, as on the ISS, written as about 0 g.
O₂ partial pressure
Oxygen fraction multiplied by total pressure; it is what matters for flammability.

Educational index, not a NASA measurement. How is it calculated?

The index is a hand-built formula: 25 + 70 × (O₂ ÷ 21 − 1) + 10 × (O₂ partial pressure − 3.09), limited to 0–100. It uses only the oxygen concentration and the oxygen partial pressure (which includes total pressure). It was not derived from NASA experiments or validated against them, and it has no uncertainty analysis.

Gravity, materials, airflow, ignition sources and detection are not part of it. Do not read it as a measured value or as a prediction of real spacecraft fire risk.

Information & foundations

The science behind the dashboard, the incidents that motivate it, and the NASA sources every summary traces back to.

Why fire in space is different

On Earth, hot gases rise because of buoyancy, which gives a candle its flickering teardrop shape. In free fall (microgravity) there is no buoyancy and no rising of hot gases, so a candle flame often becomes a dim, bluish, nearly spherical flame that relies on diffusion to get the oxygen it needs. This is a common introductory picture, not a rule: shape, color, temperature and stability depend on the fuel, oxygen concentration, pressure, airflow and combustion chemistry.

NASA’s work on spacecraft fire safety focuses on prevention, detection and extinguishment. Prevention means controlling ignition sources and selecting materials carefully, which requires understanding flammability limits. Flammability depends strongly on oxygen concentration, and in microgravity airflow also plays an important role.

The atmospheres astronauts will breathe

To make spacesuits easier to use, the future lunar base and pressurized rovers are expected to have more oxygen and lower pressure than conditions on Earth. Spacesuits are filled with 100% oxygen at only 4.3 psi, while pressure at sea level on Earth is 14.7 psi. Astronauts pre-breathe 100% oxygen to flush nitrogen from their bodies and avoid decompression sickness. To shorten that pre-breathe and make lunar excursions easier, the lunar base and pressurized vehicles are expected to sit between a spacesuit and Earth’s atmosphere. NASA’s current nominal exploration atmosphere is 34% oxygen at 8.2 psi total pressure (about 2.79 psi of oxygen), roughly 60% more oxygen by concentration than Earth’s air at 21% (NASA exploration atmospheres briefing). Earlier studies considered other mixes, such as 30% oxygen at 10.2 psi, which remain possible scenarios, and NASA notes that the Artemis habitat atmosphere has not been finalized. More oxygen makes materials much more flammable.

How NASA studies it

  • Flammability of solids such as fabrics and plastics: BASS and BASS-II, and the Saffire series. Saffire-I to -III ran at normal Cygnus cabin conditions, while Saffire-IV to -VI used reduced pressure and extra oxygen to mimic exploration atmospheres.
  • Detection: SAME and SAME-R tested smoke detection in microgravity.
  • Extinguishment: FLEX diluted the chamber atmosphere with carbon dioxide and helium, both able to smother fire.
  • High oxygen: the highest concentrations in microgravity were tested in CFI-G, part of ACME, with up to 85% oxygen.

The main NASA fire experiments in microgravity

FLEX (Flame Extinguishment Experiment)

Investigated how liquid fuel droplets burn in microgravity, using heptane and methanol. In these experiments the flames often took a spherical shape and could burn with less oxygen and at lower temperatures than on Earth, depending on the fuel, oxygen concentration, pressure and diluent. Learn more: NASA video and overview · NASA results report.

ACME (Active Control of Flames)

A set of six experiments devoted to understanding how various gases burn without the influence of gravity, in order to improve combustion efficiency and reduce emissions. Learn more: NASA ACME page.

BASS / BASS-II (Burning and Suppression of Solids)

Analyzed how fire spreads across different solids under different airflows. Learn more: NASA BASS-II article · NASA BASS-II summary report.

SAME / SAME-R (Smoke and Aerosol Measurement Experiment)

Evaluated smoke and aerosol detection systems in a microgravity environment. Learn more: NASA Glenn SAME-R page.

Saffire series (Spacecraft Fire Safety Experiment)

Objective: to study how a large fire spreads in a real spacecraft environment, simulating the conditions of future crewed missions to the Moon or Mars. Learn more: NASA Saffire overview.

Why it matters: real incidents

Apollo 1 (1967) was a real cabin fire: three astronauts died during a pre-launch test in a pure-oxygen cabin at above sea-level pressure. Apollo 13 (1970) is a different kind of event: an oxygen tank ruptured on the way to the Moon and severely damaged the spacecraft. It was an oxygen-system failure, not a fire in the crew cabin, although investigators traced it to damaged wire insulation igniting inside the high-pressure oxygen tank. The 1997 Mir fire was a real on-board fire, caused by a solid-fuel oxygen generator, and the multinational crew survived it.

During the Artemis II mission a smoke detector alarm went off. The accounts I found describe an alarm event, not a confirmed fire, and the commander called it tense but not scary (Aerospace America). It is still a modern example of why reliable detection matters. The NASA sources below cover Apollo 1, Apollo 13 and Mir.

What the data does not cover yet

PSI’s combustion collection may not contain partial-gravity datasets, but that does not mean lunar-gravity evidence is missing: NASA has already produced some outside that collection. The Lunar-g Combustion Investigation (LUCI) flew on a Blue Origin New Shepard capsule on 4 February 2025. The capsule spun to simulate about one-sixth of Earth’s gravity, and it burned cotton-fiberglass fabric and an acrylic rod in air at normal pressure. These were the first extended burns in simulated lunar gravity, and in them the flames spread more slowly than in normal gravity, so these tests did not show lunar gravity to be more flammable. Spinning adds Coriolis effects, only two materials were tested, and no Mars-gravity or habitat-atmosphere data came out of it. FM2 (Flammability of Materials on the Moon) is a separate investigation intended to run on the lunar surface, burning four samples in habitat-type atmospheres. NASA gives a planned launch of late 2026, and it has no results yet. E-FIELD Flames applies electric fields to the ions naturally present in flames to control their shape, soot and stability. It was not designed to simulate partial gravity, so it does not fill this gap. Results from microgravity and normal gravity can still help bound what happens at intermediate levels. Rocket and propellant fire safety is generally outside the scope of PSI research.

Applications on Earth

Water mist fire extinguishers

NASA has supported the development of a Fine Water Mist Portable Fire Extinguisher for spacecraft. The ISS carbon dioxide extinguisher creates an unsafe breathing environment, because the station’s emergency breathing equipment does not filter CO₂, while fine water mist can put out a fire without that hazard. NASA’s technical papers describe its development, testing and intended ISS use, so it should not be read as a standard operational ISS extinguisher. Water mist systems are also used for fire protection on Earth.

Smoke detection

A 1970s Space Shuttle fire detector already told smoke from dust to avoid nuisance alarms, and now also works in industry. Later ISS work (SAME and SAME-R) studies how smoke behaves in microgravity to improve detectors for future spacecraft.

Conclusions: fire precautions for human space exploration

What the research and the dashboard point to when planning for crews on the Moon, Mars and in spacecraft.

Bottom line. Fire in space is not simply Earth fire without gravity. Flames behave differently, the planned habitat atmospheres hold more oxygen, and the research that tells us how materials, smoke and extinguishers behave there is still incomplete. Safety depends on prevention first, then reliable detection, then suppression matched to the atmosphere.

Treat high-oxygen habitats as higher risk

NASA’s nominal exploration atmosphere is 34% oxygen at 8.2 psi, about 60% more oxygen than Earth’s air by concentration, and the challenge brief states that more oxygen makes materials much more flammable. The final Artemis habitat atmosphere has not been fixed, so other mixes such as 30% at 10.2 psi remain possible.

With the dashboard’s own calculation, 34% O₂ at 8.2 psi has an oxygen partial pressure of about 2.79 psi, a little below Earth’s 3.09 psi. The lower pressure only partly offsets the extra oxygen, because flammability depends strongly on oxygen concentration.

Select and test materials for the real atmosphere

Prevention rests on controlling ignition sources and choosing materials carefully, which requires knowing their flammability limits. Materials should be judged at the oxygen and pressure planned for the habitat, not at Earth conditions.

Do not assume less airflow means less risk

In microgravity airflow plays an important role in flammability. In BASS, with low airflow, some materials ignited at lower oxygen levels than on Earth.

Ventilation settings should therefore be part of any material safety assessment for crewed spaces.

Detect fire with sensors built for space

SAME found that smoke particles in microgravity differ from those on Earth, which affects detectors. Detection should be validated for those particles rather than assumed to work as it does on the ground.

Earlier NASA work points the same way: a 1970s shuttle fire detector that tells smoke from dust cut false alarms on Earth, and SAME now extends that knowledge to microgravity smoke.

Match suppression to the atmosphere

FLEX diluted the chamber atmosphere with carbon dioxide and helium to put flames out, which shows that changing the gas mix is one way to suppress fire in a sealed habitat.

Fine water mist is another direction. NASA has supported a portable extinguisher that can put out a fire without the CO₂-rich breathing environment of a carbon dioxide extinguisher, but NASA’s papers describe its development and testing, not routine ISS use.

Build on the partial-gravity evidence before surface operations

LUCI (2025) gave the first extended burns in simulated lunar gravity: slower flame spread than in normal gravity for a fabric and an acrylic rod, in air at normal pressure. That is useful, but it is only two materials, in one atmosphere, on a spinning capsule with Coriolis effects, and there is still no Mars-gravity data.

FM2 is planned to test four materials on the Moon in habitat-type atmospheres, and until it reports, planners should combine LUCI with microgravity and normal-gravity results and treat conclusions for surface habitats with caution. E-FIELD Flames studies electric-field control of flames and is not a partial-gravity test.
